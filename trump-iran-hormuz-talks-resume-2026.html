Trump Opens Door to Resuming Iran Talks — One Day After Ordering Them Cut Off — and Claims Full Control of Hormuz

In a dramatic reversal, President Trump told reporters talks with Tehran could restart, just 24 hours after ordering his aides to sever contact with Iranian negotiators. He repeated his claim that the United States holds full control of the Strait of Hormuz — a waterway Iran effectively closed to commercial traffic since the fighting began in late February, disrupting a critical global oil transit route. Trump's stated priority remains preventing Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons.

19 August 2026 Washington DC · Strait of Hormuz 5 min read

Twenty-four hours is a long time in the Trump presidency. On Monday, the President told reporters that talks with Iran could resume — just one day after ordering his aides to cut off all contact with Iranian negotiators. The diplomatic whiplash underscores the degree to which the Iran crisis remains a live, evolving decision space in the White House, with no settled strategy and multiple competing impulses pulling in opposite directions.

The Reversal

Asked whether he might renew negotiations, Trump responded simply: "Maybe at some point." He described the current situation with Iran as "good" but acknowledged the Strait of Hormuz remains a central point of tension. The about-face came without explanation and with no indication that Tehran had done anything in the intervening hours to alter the calculus. The most plausible reading is that the order to cut ties was either a negotiating tactic — designed to increase perceived American resolve — or a decision that was reconsidered overnight.

The Hormuz Claim

Trump repeated his standing assertion that the United States holds "possession" and "full control" of the Strait of Hormuz. The claim is operationally ambitious. Iran has effectively closed the strait to commercial shipping since fighting began in late February 2026, disrupting one of the world's most critical oil transit chokepoints. The US Navy maintains a powerful presence in the strait, but "full control" implies an ability to guarantee safe passage for commercial traffic — something the current insurance market suggests has not been achieved.

Iran continues to launch drones into the strait periodically. Trump acknowledged this obliquely, noting that Tehran "every once in a while" sends a drone. His framing — minimising the disruption while claiming total control — reflects a pattern of public messaging designed to project strength while the actual operational picture remains contested.

The Nuclear Dimension

Trump reiterated that his overriding priority is preventing Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons. This has been his stated position throughout the crisis, and it provides the framework within which all other decisions — military strikes, diplomatic overtures, sanctions escalation — are ostensibly being evaluated. The question is whether the on-again, off-again diplomacy signals genuine strategic ambiguity designed to keep Tehran guessing, or simply incoherence.

The pattern of contradictory statements within a single news cycle — sever contact on Sunday, reopen the door on Monday — creates a fog of intention that can serve as either deterrence or an invitation to miscalculation, depending on how Tehran interprets the signals.

What It Means

For allies in the Eastern Mediterranean and the Gulf — including Greece, which has Patriot batteries deployed in Saudi Arabia and F-16s in Cyprus — the Trump administration's oscillating posture introduces a layer of planning uncertainty. Military deployments are built on assumptions about political direction. When the political direction reverses within hours, the forces on the ground must maintain readiness for every scenario simultaneously, which is exactly what the Hellenic National Defence General Staff reported doing earlier today.

The Strait of Hormuz remains the fulcrum. If Trump's claim of full control is aspirational rather than actual, then the diplomatic channel he has just reopened may be the only mechanism for de-escalating the single greatest threat to global energy security. If it is real, then the leverage for a deal exists — but only for as long as Washington is willing to sustain the naval presence required to enforce it.
